## Executive Summary

JEPI is an equity-income ETF designed to provide monthly distributable income while maintaining exposure to U.S. large-cap stocks. Its defining trade-off is lower expected volatility in exchange for giving up part of the upside that a plain S&P 500 holding may capture during strong rallies.

The fund has a longer live history than the newer option-income products in this series. That history makes JEPI useful for studying how an income overlay behaved during the 2022 equity drawdown, but it does not make the fund a guaranteed bear-market hedge.

## Strategy and Business Model

JPMorgan describes JEPI as a combination of a diversified, lower-volatility equity portfolio and option selling. Premiums and stock dividends support the monthly distribution objective. The structure is materially different from simply holding the S&P 500: the equity portfolio may have a different sector and factor profile, while the option overlay can reduce participation in sharp upside moves.

The March 31, 2026 fact sheet lists a 0.35% annual expense ratio and a May 20, 2020 class launch. These are fund-level facts, not forecasts of future yield or return.

## Evidence from the 2022 Bear Market

The existing study records JEPI's 2022 total return at approximately -3.52% versus -18.32% for the S&P 500 over the same calendar year. That is meaningful evidence of relative downside resilience, but it should be read with two qualifications: one observation is not a permanent guarantee, and calendar-year comparisons can hide the timing of distributions, drawdowns, and recoveries.

The more defensible conclusion is that JEPI's design can produce a different return path from the benchmark. Investors should compare total return, maximum drawdown, volatility, and the distribution's tax classification together rather than treating the cash distribution as free return.

## Distribution and Tax Context

JEPI's monthly distribution is not identical to dividend income from the underlying stocks. It can reflect option premiums, dividends, realized gains, and other components. The final classification may change by year and must be checked against official tax documents. A distribution rate should therefore be treated as a historical observation, not a promised rate.

## Key Risks

- Selling options can limit upside participation during sustained rallies.
- The equity portfolio may differ from the S&P 500 in sector exposure, factor exposure, and security selection.
- Equity-linked notes and derivatives introduce implementation and counterparty considerations.
- A high monthly distribution does not guarantee positive total return or preservation of NAV.
- Historical 2022 downside resilience may not repeat in a different volatility or interest-rate regime.

## Analyst Conclusion

JEPI is best understood as a mature equity-income strategy with useful live bear-market evidence, not as a substitute for a cash allocation or a guaranteed hedge. Its strongest case is for readers who value recurring distributions and are willing to accept reduced upside participation. The conclusion should be revisited whenever the portfolio, option implementation, or distribution composition changes.

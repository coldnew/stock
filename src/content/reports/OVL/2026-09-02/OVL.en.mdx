## Executive Summary

OVL changes the shape of large-cap equity risk through a put-spread overlay. Unlike a covered-call fund, it does not primarily exchange upside participation for option income. The fund maintains broad U.S. large-cap exposure and sells short-term put spreads to generate cash flow. That structure leaves more upside available, but the option position can add to losses when the equity market falls.

The six-year analysis through 2026-09-02 shows this trade-off clearly. OVL's estimated upside beta versus SPY was 1.022, while its downside beta was 1.101. In other words, the fund participated slightly more than the benchmark on rising days and materially more on falling days. The result is not a defensive income profile. It is an equity allocation with an income overlay that has historically amplified downside in exchange for preserving upside participation.

## Strategy and Business Model

Liquid Strategies describes OVL as an actively managed ETF that seeks capital appreciation and income through large-cap equity exposure and an options overlay. The core exposure is primarily obtained through broad-market equity investments, while the adviser sells and purchases listed short-term put options. The distance between the put strikes is determined by the adviser's assessment of expected volatility before expiration.

The practical difference from a covered call is important. A covered call receives premium but can cap part of a rally. A put spread can leave the upside relatively open, but selling downside protection creates a liability when the market drops through the relevant strikes. Premium income is compensation for accepting that risk, not a guaranteed buffer. The analysis data showed an expense ratio of approximately 0.79% and assets of about $428 million.

OVL also changed its distribution schedule in 2026. From its 2019 launch through 2025, the fund generally paid quarterly distributions with a much lower annualized rate. It shifted to monthly distributions in January 2026, and the recent annualized distribution rate rose above 10%. The monthly history is still short, so the new cash-flow policy has not yet been tested through a full market cycle.

## Evidence

OVL has a valuable longer record than many newer option-income ETFs. During the 2020 COVID-market crash, OVL fell approximately 35.5% while SPY fell 32.4%, an estimated downside capture of 1.10. OVL reached its low in about 30 days and recovered in approximately 145 days, for a total recovery period of roughly 175 days.

The 2022 bear market was slower and more revealing. OVL declined 31.2% from its prior high while SPY declined 25.4%, producing an estimated downside capture of 1.23. The decline lasted about 291 days and the recovery took approximately 504 days, for a total period of roughly 795 days. This is the central risk of the put-spread design: the fund can eventually recover and still impose a long waiting period on an investor who needs stable capital or cash flow during the drawdown.

The long-term NAV record is more constructive than the drawdown record. After the 2020 low, OVL rose approximately 203.5%; after the 2022 low, it rose approximately 98.6%. Those observations show that downside amplification did not prevent later recovery in the periods examined. They do not establish that every future decline will recover on the same schedule.

Distributions were not protected during either bear market. The payment declined by roughly 52% during the 2020 crash and about 51% during the 2022 decline. A rising volatility environment may increase option premium, but a falling asset base can still reduce the dollars available for distribution. A high current distribution rate should therefore be treated as variable cash flow, not as a fixed coupon.

## Key Risks

- Put selling can amplify losses during a prolonged or abrupt equity decline. OVL's historical downside beta above one is direct evidence of this exposure.
- The monthly distribution regime began only in 2026. Its long-term sustainability and behavior during a future bear market remain untested.
- Distributions may include return of capital and are not guaranteed. Cash received during a drawdown may coincide with reduced NAV and reduced future income capacity.
- The fund has equity, derivatives, liquidity, counterparty, and implementation risks. Option spreads can behave differently when markets gap or volatility changes quickly.
- Historical recovery after 2020 and 2022 does not guarantee a comparable recovery time. Investors may experience multi-year periods of underperformance or reduced cash flow.

## Analyst Conclusion

OVL is not a conventional high-income defensive ETF. Its historical profile is closer to large-cap equity with a put-based income overlay: upside beta remained near or slightly above one, while downside beta was higher. The strategy preserved more upside than covered-call peers in the periods examined, but it paid for that feature by accepting deeper losses during both of the major bear markets in the record.

The product may be relevant for an investor who values broad equity exposure and current cash flow, understands that distributions can fall, and can tolerate amplified drawdowns. It is a poor match for an investor who interprets a 10% distribution rate as a capital-protection feature. The strongest conclusion supported by the data is conditional: OVL recovered from prior bear markets, but it did so after larger drawdowns and, in 2022, a recovery process lasting more than two years. The put-spread income should be evaluated as payment for accepting downside risk, not as evidence that downside risk has been removed.

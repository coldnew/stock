## Executive Summary

SPYI is the largest and longest-running fund in this report series, but size is not a guarantee of income or capital protection. The fund combines S&P 500 exposure with SPX index-option positions designed to generate monthly income and retain some equity appreciation. Its structure differs from a simple covered call: NEOS describes using both sold and purchased index options, which can create a call-spread profile rather than a single short-call overlay.

Through 2026-09-03, SPYI's estimated average annual return since inception was 14.83%, and its one-year total return was approximately 17.48%. Its estimated beta versus the S&P 500 was 0.71. These are useful historical observations, but they do not show whether the lower beta came from genuine downside protection, lower participation on both rising and falling days, or the interaction of the option positions with the market path. The available record still lacks a complete deep-bear-market test.

## Strategy and Business Model

NEOS states that SPYI seeks high monthly income with potential upside appreciation in rising markets. The fund invests in S&P 500 constituents and implements a data-driven strategy using sold and purchased SPX index options. SPX options are index contracts rather than options written directly on every share held by the fund. NEOS also highlights Section 1256 treatment and possible tax-loss harvesting, but those features do not determine the complete tax result for an individual investor.

SPYI charges a 0.68% management fee and pays monthly. The call-spread construction can be more flexible than selling calls against the entire equity portfolio, because purchased calls can offset some upside that would otherwise be surrendered. The trade-off is that option spreads, timing, and volatility assumptions make the return path less transparent than a passive S&P 500 fund. The correct question is not whether the fund distributes cash, but how much total return remains after producing that cash flow.

## Evidence

SPYI's recent monthly payments were approximately $0.51 to $0.54 per share over the observed eight-month sample. The coefficient of variation was about 1.7%, indicating a narrow recent range. That statistic covers a relatively calm period and should not be compared directly with full-history measures for funds whose samples include major corrections. Distribution schedules and amounts can change when volatility, option positioning, and NAV change.

The fund's 0.71 beta is lower than the estimated betas of QQQI and GPIQ, but beta alone is not a downside measure. Without issuer-published up-day and down-day beta splits, the available data cannot establish whether SPYI lost less during declines while keeping more of the S&P 500's gains, or whether it simply compressed both directions. This is a material disclosure limitation when evaluating the fund as a defensive income allocation.

Distribution quality is another important distinction. The observed payout-to-earnings ratio was approximately 308.78%, meaning distributions were more than three times the reported accounting earnings used in that comparison. This strongly suggests ordinary company earnings do not by themselves account for the cash payments. NEOS states that distributions may include option premiums, dividends, capital gains, interest, and return of capital. ROC can reduce an investor's cost basis and may have different withholding implications, but it is not automatically new economic profit. The issuer's 19a-1 notices and annual tax forms are the authoritative documents for each distribution.

SPYI began in August 2022. Its record includes recovery and rising-market conditions, but not a full, prolonged deep bear market after launch. A lower beta may reduce volatility in some periods, yet the option structure can still produce lower distributions or weaker participation when the market falls for many months. Historical income stability during a calm window is not a guarantee of stability during a stress event.

## Key Risks

- The available post-launch record does not provide a complete deep-bear-market stress test. A beta of 0.71 cannot establish the size of future losses.
- Call spreads and index options can reduce or reshape upside participation. SPYI may lag the S&P 500 in a strong rally even when its distribution rate is high.
- A payout-to-earnings ratio above 300% is a warning against treating the distribution as ordinary earnings. ROC may reduce cost basis and is not guaranteed to continue.
- Monthly payments can vary with option premiums, realized gains, NAV, and portfolio decisions. A stable recent range may not survive a prolonged decline.
- Index derivatives introduce liquidity, counterparty, basis, implementation, and tax-reporting risks. Section 1256 treatment does not remove all investor-level tax obligations.

## Analyst Conclusion

SPYI is a large-scale S&P 500 income vehicle with a more complex index-option design than a basic covered-call ETF. Its historical average return, recent distribution range, and 0.71 beta are constructive, but they do not support describing the fund as a proven capital-preservation product. The important unresolved question is how the call-spread structure behaves in a slow, deep equity decline and whether monthly cash flow remains useful when the NAV base is shrinking.

SPYI is most defensible for an investor who wants S&P 500 exposure with current cash flow, accepts that the payment is variable, and evaluates the position using total return and NAV rather than yield alone. The lower beta should be treated as a historical description, not a promise of downside protection. A future review should examine up-day and down-day participation separately, distribution classifications, and whether NAV growth keeps pace with cash paid out. The large asset base improves liquidity context; it does not remove the trade-off between current income and uncertain future participation.
